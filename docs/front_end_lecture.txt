GNSS Front End
Redesigned Undergraduate Lecture Content

From the Received GNSS Signal to Digital Samples

Audience: BS Space Science students with no prior background in electronics.

The lecture will follow one GPS L1 signal through the complete receiver front end. The content follows the existing undergraduate lecture and 6. GPS Front End.pdf, using their concepts, equations, examples, and reference frequencies.

Every major section follows the same teaching sequence: definition → physical explanation → example → signal in the time domain → signal in the frequency domain → glossary.

A fundamental rule throughout the lecture is that the signal remains analog until it reaches the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). No raw I/Q files, digital samples, or histograms should appear immediately after the RF filter.

Section 01 — Understanding the GNSS Signal
Slide 01 — Introduction to the GNSS Front End
Definition

A GNSS front end is the part of a satellite navigation receiver that receives, filters, amplifies, converts, and digitizes weak radio-frequency signals transmitted by navigation satellites.

The front end does not calculate the receiver's position. Its purpose is to prepare the received signals for subsequent digital signal processing.

Learning objectives

Students should understand how a GNSS signal changes as it travels through the receiver and be able to identify the function of each front-end component.
Slide 02 — What Is a GNSS Radio Signal?
Definition

A GNSS radio signal is an electromagnetic wave transmitted by a navigation satellite. It carries information that allows a receiver to determine satellite-related timing and navigation parameters.

Three basic quantities describe the received electrical signal:
- Amplitude: The magnitude of the signal, represented by the height of its waveform.
- Frequency: The number of complete oscillations occurring in one second.
- Time: The quantity used to describe when the waveform changes or when measurements are taken.
Visual example

Show a simple sinusoidal waveform with time on the horizontal axis and amplitude on the vertical axis. Label one complete cycle.

This waveform is an introductory illustration of frequency, not a representation of the complete received GNSS signal.
Slide 03 — Understanding the Time and Frequency Domains
Time domain

A time-domain graph shows how the amplitude of a signal changes with time.

A sinusoidal signal appears as a repeating waveform. A real received GNSS signal normally appears irregular because the satellite signal is extremely weak and is mixed with noise.

Frequency domain

A frequency-domain graph shows how the signal's power is distributed over different frequencies.

For a GNSS signal, the frequency-domain representation identifies the carrier frequency, signal bandwidth, and unwanted frequency components.

Conceptual representations of a single sinusoidal signal. Actual GNSS signals have a spread spectrum rather than a single narrow spectral line.Slide 04 — GPS L1 Carrier Frequency
Definition

Carrier frequency is the frequency of the radio wave used to transmit the GNSS signal.

The GPS L1 carrier frequency is:
\[
f_{L1}=1575.42\text{ MHz}
\]

A frequency of 1575.42 MHz corresponds to approximately 1.575 billion cycles every second.

Example

The corresponding wavelength is:
\[
\lambda=\frac{c}{f}
\]

\[
\lambda=\frac{3\times10^8}{1575.42\times10^6}
\approx 0.1903\text{ m}
\]

Therefore, the GPS L1 wavelength is approximately 19 cm.
Slide 05 — How Information Is Carried by the GPS Signal
The GPS L1 C/A signal contains three important elements.

Carrier: The high-frequency wave transmitted at 1575.42 MHz.

PRN ranging code: A known sequence of chips used to distinguish satellite signals and determine signal propagation delay. The GPS C/A code rate is 1.023 million chips per second.

Navigation data: Digital information describing satellite orbits, clock parameters, and related navigation information. The traditional GPS L1 C/A navigation data rate is 50 bits per second.

These elements are combined through modulation.
Slide 06 — GPS Signal Modulation
Definition

Modulation is the process of changing a carrier property to carry information.

The GPS L1 C/A signal uses binary phase-shift keying (BPSK-like code modulation), in which the carrier phase changes by either 0° or 180°.

Example

Consider the sequence of code chips:
\[
+1,\;-1,\;+1
\]

The first chip preserves the reference carrier phase. The second reverses its sign, corresponding to a 180° phase change. The third restores the original sign.

Visual example

Show three successive portions of a carrier waveform, with the middle portion inverted. Place the three chip values directly above the waveform.
Slide 07 — Understanding GPS L1 Signal Bandwidth
Definition

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies occupied by a signal or passed by a receiver component.

The GPS L1 C/A signal has a chip rate of 1.023 MHz. Its approximate main-lobe null-to-null bandwidth is:
\[
BW=2(1.023)=2.046\text{ MHz}
\]

This main lobe extends approximately from:
\[
1574.397\text{ MHz to }1576.443\text{ MHz}
\]

Important distinction

The 2.046 MHz value describes the central main lobe, not the complete GPS L1 C/A spectrum. The complete spread-spectrum signal also contains weaker side lobes extending beyond the first nulls.

Visual example

Show the complete central main lobe and progressively smaller side lobes. Label the carrier frequency, the first two nulls, and the 2.046 MHz main-lobe width.
Slide 08 — Why the GPS Signal Is Extremely Weak
The satellite signal travels a very large distance before reaching the receiver. As a result, the received power is extremely small.

The source lecture uses a minimum GPS received signal power of approximately:
\[
P_r=-160\text{ dBW}=-130\text{ dBm}
\]

For a receiver bandwidth of 2 MHz at approximately 290 K, the thermal noise power is:
\[
N=kTB
\]

\[
N\approx-111\text{ dBm}
\]

Therefore, the example GPS signal is approximately 19 dB below the integrated thermal noise power.

Interpretation

The received signal cannot normally be distinguished from thermal noise simply by observing the time-domain waveform.

A spectrum analyzer may also show noise dominating the weak GPS signal.
Slide 09 — Visual Example: The Received Signal and Thermal Noise
Show two representations of the same incoming signal.

Time domain: An irregular waveform dominated by thermal noise, with no clearly identifiable satellite code.

Frequency domain: A broad noise floor with the desired GPS spectrum lying below or close to it. The central signal spectrum may not be directly visible in an ordinary short observation.

The explanation should emphasize that the satellite signal is still present, even when its waveform cannot be distinguished visually.

Slide 11 — What Happens to the Signal Inside the Receiver?
The front end performs the following operations in sequence.
Operation	Purpose
Reception	Convert the arriving radio wave into an electrical signal
RF Filtering	Attenuate unwanted frequencies
Amplification	Increase signal magnitude while limiting added noise
Frequency Translation	Shift the signal to a lower frequency
IF Filtering and Amplification	Control bandwidth and prepare signal amplitude
Sampling	Measure the waveform at regular time intervals
Quantization	Represent each measured amplitude using a finite number of levels

The first five operations occur while the signal remains analog. Sampling and quantization take place in the ADC.

Section 03 — GNSS Receiving Antenna
Slide 12 — Function of the GNSS Antenna
Definition

A GNSS receiving antenna converts incident electromagnetic waves into a weak electrical voltage that can enter the receiver's RF circuitry.

The antenna does not identify satellites or calculate coordinates.

It receives the combined radio signals arriving from satellites within its supported frequency bands.

Example

A receiver simultaneously receives signals from four GPS satellites.

All four signals are collected by the same antenna. The receiver front end processes the combined signal, and the digital processing stages later distinguish the individual satellites using their PRN codes.
Slide 13 — Antenna Polarization
Definition

Polarization describes the orientation and movement of the electric field of a radio wave.

GNSS satellites generally transmit right-hand circularly polarized (RHCP) signals. GNSS receiving antennas are designed for corresponding polarization.

Example

A properly matched RHCP receiving antenna receives the direct GNSS signal more effectively than an ideal antenna designed only for the opposite circular polarization.

The matching of polarization reduces power loss.

Visual example

Display an RHCP wave with the electric-field vector rotating along the direction of propagation.
Slide 14 — Antenna Gain and Radiation Pattern
Antenna gain describes how strongly the antenna receives signals from a particular direction.

Radiation pattern describes how this reception varies with direction.

A typical sky-facing GNSS patch antenna is designed to receive signals from satellites above the horizon while reducing reception from unwanted directions.

Example

Compare a satellite directly overhead with one close to the horizon. Their signals may experience different antenna gains because the antenna response varies with elevation.

Visual example

Show a sky-facing antenna with a broad reception region above it and weaker reception toward the ground.
Slide 15 — Multi-Constellation and Multi-Band Reception
Multi-constellation reception means receiving and processing signals from more than one satellite navigation system.

GPS L1, Galileo E1 and BeiDou B1C share the nominal center frequency of 1575.42 MHz.

Multi-band reception means supporting different frequency bands, such as GPS L1 at 1575.42 MHz and GPS L5 at 1176.45 MHz.

Multi-channel tracking means that the receiver's digital processor can track several satellite signals independently. It does not mean that each satellite requires its own antenna.

Example

An L1-capable antenna receives signals from GPS and Galileo satellites together. After the front end digitizes the composite signal, different digital tracking channels identify and follow their respective satellite signals.
Slide 16 — Active GNSS Antenna
Definition

An active GNSS antenna includes a low-noise amplifier (LNA), which increases the weak received signal before substantial cable loss occurs.

The reason for placing amplification near the antenna is to protect the received signal quality.

Worked example

The lecture uses an antenna amplifier with 30 dB gain and a cable with 8 dB loss.

Assume the received signal power is −130 dBm.
\[
P_{\mathrm{after\ LNA}}=-130+30=-100\text{ dBm}
\]

After the cable:
\[
P_{\mathrm{after\ cable}}=-100-8=-108\text{ dBm}
\]

The cable reduces signal power, but the early amplifier gain makes the cable's noise contribution less significant than if the cable were placed ahead of the LNA.
Slide 17 — Signal Condition After the GNSS Antenna
Time-domain condition

The antenna produces an extremely weak analog electrical waveform. The waveform contains satellite signals, noise, and possible reflected signals.

It does not resemble a clean sinusoidal signal in ordinary observation.

Frequency-domain condition

The received spectrum is centered around the supported GNSS frequencies. For the L1 example, the desired signal is centered at 1575.42 MHz, with its spread-spectrum bandwidth surrounding that frequency.

The signal may remain below the thermal noise floor.

Visual example

Left: irregular low-amplitude analog waveform.

Right: noise spectrum with the weak L1 band marked at its correct frequency.

Section 04 — RF Bandpass Filter
Slide 18 — Function of the RF Bandpass Filter
Definition

An RF bandpass filter is a frequency-selective component that passes signals within a desired frequency range while reducing signals outside that range.

The received antenna signal may contain strong unwanted radio transmissions. These should be attenuated before they enter the high-gain receiver stages.

Important point

The RF filter does not separate individual GPS satellites. It selects a frequency band containing their combined signals.
Slide 19 — Understanding Filter Bandwidth
A practical bandpass filter is described by its center frequency, bandwidth, insertion loss, and selectivity.

The reference RF filter uses:
\[
f_c=1575.42\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
BW_{3dB}\approx50\text{ MHz}
\]

For a symmetric passband, the approximate lower and upper 3-dB frequencies are 1550.42 MHz and 1600.42 MHz.

Example

The GPS L1 signal falls within this passband, while radio signals sufficiently far outside it are strongly attenuated.

The transition is gradual: a real filter cannot perfectly pass every wanted frequency and completely block every unwanted frequency.
Slide 20 — Insertion Loss of a Filter
Definition

Insertion loss is the reduction of wanted signal power caused by the filter itself.

Even within the passband, a practical filter introduces some attenuation.

Worked example

Suppose the signal entering a filter has power −130 dBm and the filter has 1.5 dB insertion loss.
\[
P_{\mathrm{out}}=-130-1.5=-131.5\text{ dBm}
\]

This means that filtering improves frequency selection but may also reduce the power of the useful GNSS signal.
Slide 21 — Signal Condition After RF Filtering
Time-domain condition

The output is still a continuous analog waveform. Unwanted out-of-band energy has been reduced, but the signal does not automatically become a recognizable GPS waveform.

Frequency-domain condition

The spectrum outside the filter's passband is attenuated, while the desired L1 signal spectrum remains within the passed frequency range.

The center frequency is still 1575.42 MHz.

Visual example

Use two frequency-domain plots, before and after filtering, with the same horizontal and vertical scales. Show strong unwanted frequency components being reduced while the wanted L1 band remains.

Underneath, use two corresponding analog time-domain waveforms. Do not display digital samples or a data file at this stage.

Section 05 — Amplification and Noise Figure
Slide 22 — Why Amplification Is Necessary
Definition

Amplification is the process of increasing the magnitude or power of an electrical signal.

Because the received GNSS signal is extremely weak, amplification is necessary before digitization.

An amplifier increases both the desired signal and the noise already present at its input. Practical amplifiers also add some internal noise.
Slide 23 — Understanding Amplifier Gain
Definition

Gain is the ratio of output power to input power.

When power is expressed in dBm, gain in dB can be added directly:
\[
G_{\mathrm{dB}}=P_{\mathrm{out,dBm}}-P_{\mathrm{in,dBm}}
\]

Worked example

An amplifier receives −110 dBm and provides 20 dB gain.
\[
P_{\mathrm{out}}=-110+20=-90\text{ dBm}
\]

The output power is greater than the input power.

However, amplification does not by itself separate the weak GPS signal from thermal noise.
Slide 24 — Understanding Signal-to-Noise Ratio
Definition

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compares the power of a wanted signal with the noise power in the same measurement bandwidth.
\[
SNR=\frac{P_{\mathrm{signal}}}{P_{\mathrm{noise}}}
\]

A higher SNR means that the signal is stronger relative to the noise.

Example

If both signal and existing noise are amplified by the same amount, their ratio does not improve. If the amplifier adds extra noise, the output SNR becomes worse.

Visual example

Show two time-domain waveforms with different vertical amplitudes but the same relative noise appearance. Show the corresponding frequency spectra with both signal and noise power raised.
Slide 25 — Noise Figure
Definition

Noise figure describes how much a receiver component degrades the signal-to-noise ratio.

A lower noise figure indicates less additional degradation.

Worked example

A component receives a signal with an input SNR of 10 dB and produces an output SNR of 7 dB.
\[
NF=10-7=3\text{ dB}
\]

Therefore, the component has introduced a 3 dB deterioration in SNR.
Slide 26 — Why the First Amplifier Is Important
The total receiver noise figure depends strongly on the first stages in the front end.

For cascaded components, the noise factor is expressed by the Friis equation:
\[
F_{\mathrm{system}}
=
F_1+\frac{F_2-1}{G_1}
+\frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2}+\cdots
\]

Here, \(F\) represents noise factor and \(G\) represents linear power gain.

The equation shows that the noise contributed by later stages is reduced in relative importance when the first amplifier provides sufficiently high gain.

Example

Compare an 8 dB cable loss placed before an LNA with the same cable placed after a 30 dB low-noise amplifier.

The second arrangement provides better protection of receiver sensitivity.
Slide 27 — Signal Condition After Amplification
Time-domain condition

The analog voltage becomes larger, but its noise-like character remains. It is still not a decoded GNSS signal.

Frequency-domain condition

The desired GPS signal and the existing input noise both increase in power. The frequency center remains at 1575.42 MHz.

Practical amplifier noise adds to the spectrum, and no frequency translation has yet occurred.

Visual example

Show an analog waveform before and after amplification, using equal scales. Show that the amplitude increases without changing the frequency of the carrier.

On the frequency plots, show that the spectrum is raised in power without shifting along the frequency axis.

Term	Meaning

Gain	Ratio of output power to input power
SNR	Signal power relative to noise power
Noise Figure	SNR degradation introduced by a component
Noise Factor	Linear representation of noise figure

Section 06 — Mixer and Local Oscillator
Slide 28 — Why Frequency Translation Is Necessary
The received GPS L1 signal is centered at 1575.42 MHz.

For the reference receiver, processing this signal is made more practical by translating it to a lower frequency before the ADC.

Definition

Frequency translation is the process of shifting the frequency of a signal while preserving its useful modulation information.

The lower frequency produced by this operation is called the intermediate frequency (IF).
Slide 29 — Function of the Mixer
Definition

A mixer combines the incoming RF signal with a signal generated by the local oscillator (LO).

The mixing operation produces sum-frequency and difference-frequency components.
\[
f_{\mathrm{difference}}=|f_{\mathrm{RF}}-f_{\mathrm{LO}}|
\]

\[
f_{\mathrm{sum}}=f_{\mathrm{RF}}+f_{\mathrm{LO}}
\]

The desired difference-frequency component can then be selected by an IF filter.
Slide 30 — Worked Example: GPS L1 Frequency Conversion
Given
\[
f_{\mathrm{RF}}=1575.42\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
f_{\mathrm{LO}}=1527.68\text{ MHz}
\]

Difference frequency
\[
f_{\mathrm{IF}}=1575.42-1527.68
\]

\[
\boxed{f_{\mathrm{IF}}=47.74\text{ MHz}}
\]

Sum frequency
\[
f_{\mathrm{sum}}=1575.42+1527.68
\]

\[
\boxed{f_{\mathrm{sum}}=3103.10\text{ MHz}}
\]

The next filter retains the desired 47.74 MHz component while attenuating the unwanted high-frequency mixing product.
Slide 31 — Role of the Local Oscillator
Definition

A local oscillator generates the stable periodic signal used by the mixer to perform frequency translation.

In the reference front end, its frequency is 1527.68 MHz.

A stable receiver clock and frequency synthesizer are used to generate the required frequencies for the local oscillator and sampling operations.

Reference values

Receiver clock: 10 MHz

Synthesized local oscillator: 1527.68 MHz

ADC sampling frequency: 38.192 MHz

The sampling frequency is obtained in the reference design using a divider:
\[
f_s=\frac{1527.68}{40}=38.192\text{ MHz}
\]

Slide 32 — Understanding Mixer Image Frequency
Definition

An image frequency is an unwanted RF frequency that produces the same difference-frequency magnitude as the desired RF signal when mixed with the local oscillator.

For the reference design:
\[
f_{\mathrm{image}}=1527.68-47.74
\]

\[
f_{\mathrm{image}}=1479.94\text{ MHz}
\]

Both 1479.94 MHz and 1575.42 MHz can produce a 47.74 MHz difference relative to the selected local oscillator.

This is one reason why RF filtering is important before mixing.
Slide 33 — Signal Condition After Mixing
Time-domain condition

The signal remains analog. Its carrier frequency has been shifted from 1575.42 MHz to 47.74 MHz, while its ranging-code and navigation-data modulation is preserved.

In a real noise-dominated recording, the waveform continues to appear irregular.

Frequency-domain condition

Immediately after mixing, both the sum and difference components may be present.

The desired component is centered at 47.74 MHz. The unwanted sum component is centered at 3103.10 MHz.

Visual example

Show a frequency-axis diagram with an input band centered at 1575.42 MHz and the two resulting mixer components. Emphasize the desired 47.74 MHz output.

Use a separate idealized waveform to illustrate the reduced carrier frequency, with a caption stating that actual received GNSS signals remain noise-like.

Section 07 — Intermediate-Frequency Filtering and Amplification
Slide 34 — Why IF Filtering Is Required
The mixer output contains the desired intermediate-frequency signal and unwanted mixing components.

An IF bandpass filter selects the desired frequency region.

Filtering at 47.74 MHz also permits practical control of the signal bandwidth before digitization.
Slide 35 — First IF Bandpass Filter
The reference first IF filter has:
\[
f_c=47.74\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
BW\approx18\text{ MHz}
\]

Its primary role is to retain the desired IF band while attenuating unwanted mixing products and out-of-band energy.

Visual example

Show the mixer spectrum before filtering and the IF spectrum after filtering.

The 47.74 MHz band remains; the high-frequency sum product is removed.
Slide 36 — IF Amplifier
Definition

The IF amplifier increases the magnitude of the intermediate-frequency signal before the ADC.

The reference lecture describes an IF amplifier providing approximately 50 dB gain.

Like the RF amplifier, it increases the desired signal and existing noise, while adding some internal noise.

Example

An analog IF signal enters the amplifier at a low amplitude. The amplifier increases its magnitude so that the later ADC can use its available input voltage range effectively.
Slide 37 — Final IF Bandpass Filter
The reference final IF filter has:
\[
f_c=47.74\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
BW\approx6\text{ MHz}
\]

Its role is to limit the analog bandwidth before sampling, reducing unwanted frequency components and integrated noise.

The GPS L1 C/A main lobe occupies approximately 2.046 MHz, so this 6 MHz filter passes the main lobe while also retaining part of the surrounding side-lobe spectrum.
Slide 38 — Why Filters Are Used at Different Frequencies
The same narrow absolute bandwidth is generally easier to implement at a lower center frequency.

The quality factor is:
\[
Q=\frac{f_{\mathrm{center}}}{BW}
\]

For a bandwidth of 2.046 MHz at the GPS L1 RF frequency:
\[
Q_{\mathrm{RF}}=\frac{1575.42}{2.046}\approx770
\]

At the 47.74 MHz IF:
\[
Q_{\mathrm{IF}}=\frac{47.74}{2.046}\approx23.33
\]

This comparison illustrates why narrow filtering can be more practical after frequency translation.
Slide 39 — Signal Condition After IF Conditioning
Time-domain condition

The signal remains a continuous analog waveform. The signal amplitude has been adjusted, and unwanted components have been attenuated.

The original PRN modulation and navigation data are still present.

Frequency-domain condition

The desired signal is centered at 47.74 MHz, within the bandwidth selected by the final IF filter.

The frequency center does not change during IF filtering or amplification.

Visual example

Show three frequency plots using the same scales:
- After mixer: desired IF plus unwanted products.
- After first IF filter: desired IF with reduced unwanted products.
- After final IF filter: narrower passed band around 47.74 MHz.
Show a noise-like analog waveform underneath each plot, emphasizing that the signal has not yet been digitized.

Section 08 — Analog-to-Digital Converter
This section should be taught particularly carefully, because it introduces the transition from a continuous analog signal to digital numerical samples.
Slide 40 — What Is an Analog-to-Digital Converter?
Definition

An Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) is a circuit that converts a continuous analog electrical signal into a sequence of digital numbers.

The ADC performs two fundamental operations:

Sampling: Measuring the signal amplitude at selected instants of time.

Quantization: Assigning each measured amplitude to one of a finite number of digital levels.

The ADC therefore changes the representation of the signal, not its underlying satellite information.
Slide 41 — Understanding Sampling
Definition

Sampling is the process of measuring the amplitude of an analog signal at regular time intervals.

The number of samples taken each second is called the sampling frequency.
\[
f_s=\frac{1}{T_s}
\]

Here, \(T_s\) is the time interval between successive samples.

Visual example

Show a continuous analog sinusoidal curve with small dots placed at equal time intervals. Each dot represents a measured amplitude.

Explain that the dots are measurements of the original waveform, not a separate signal transmitted by the satellite.
Slide 42 — Sampling Frequency Example
The reference GPS front end uses:
\[
f_s=38.192\text{ MHz}
\]

This means:
\[
38.192\times10^6
\]

samples are taken every second.

The time interval between samples is:
\[
T_s=\frac{1}{38.192\times10^6}
\]

\[
T_s\approx26.18\text{ ns}
\]

Therefore, the ADC measures the incoming analog signal approximately once every 26.18 nanoseconds.
Slide 43 — Nyquist Frequency
Definition

The Nyquist frequency is half of the sampling frequency.
\[
f_N=\frac{f_s}{2}
\]

For the reference design:
\[
f_N=\frac{38.192}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{f_N=19.096\text{ MHz}}
\]

In ordinary sampling without intended aliasing, frequencies above the Nyquist limit can become indistinguishable from lower frequencies in the sampled representation.
Slide 44 — Understanding Aliasing
Definition

Aliasing occurs when different analog frequencies produce indistinguishable sampled-frequency representations.

This can happen when the sampling rate is insufficient to uniquely represent the analog frequencies involved.

In the reference GPS front end, aliasing is used intentionally to represent the 47.74 MHz analog IF at a lower digital frequency.

Visual example

Show two simple sinusoidal waveforms sampled at the same instants, producing the same pattern of sample points despite their different analog frequencies.

This is an introductory demonstration; the actual GPS signal is spread-spectrum and noise-dominated.
Slide 45 — Worked Example: Intentional Aliasing
Given

Analog IF:
\[
f_{\mathrm{IF}}=47.74\text{ MHz}
\]

Sampling frequency:
\[
f_s=38.192\text{ MHz}
\]

The reference design produces:
\[
f_{\mathrm{digital}}=|f_{\mathrm{IF}}-f_s|
\]

\[
f_{\mathrm{digital}}=|47.74-38.192|
\]

\[
\boxed{f_{\mathrm{digital}}=9.548\text{ MHz}}
\]

The signal's center frequency appears at 9.548 MHz in the sampled spectrum.

The desired main-lobe bandwidth remains approximately 2.046 MHz, provided that the analog filtering and sampling conditions preserve it.
Slide 46 — Signal in the Time and Frequency Domains After Sampling
Time domain

The signal is no longer represented by a continuously available voltage measurement. It is represented at discrete instants.

The recorded GPS samples still appear irregular because the input is noise dominated.

Frequency domain

The 47.74 MHz analog IF band appears at a digital frequency of 9.548 MHz.

The GPS C/A main lobe appears approximately from 8.525 MHz to 10.571 MHz.

Visual example

Display a before-and-after comparison with the analog IF spectrum centered at 47.74 MHz and the sampled spectrum centered at 9.548 MHz.

Use the same approximate 2.046 MHz main-lobe width in both plots.
Slide 47 — Understanding Quantization
Definition

Quantization is the process of representing each sampled analog amplitude using one of a fixed number of digital levels.

Analog voltage can vary continuously within the input range. Digital representation uses only a limited set of values.

The number of quantization levels is determined by the number of bits:
\[
L=2^N
\]

where \(N\) is the number of bits and \(L\) is the number of available levels.
Slide 48 — Quantization Bits and Levels
Bit Depth	Number of Levels
1 bit	2
2 bits	4
3 bits	8
4 bits	16
8 bits	256

The reference ADC has 4-bit resolution and therefore provides:
\[
2^4=16
\]

possible digital amplitude levels.

A larger number of bits allows amplitudes to be represented in smaller steps for a fixed analog input range.
Slide 49 — Visual Example: Four-Bit Quantization Bins
Definition

A quantization bin is one of the intervals into which the ADC's analog input range is divided. Each bin is associated with one digital output level.
4-Bit ADC — Sixteen Quantization Bins
The 16 bins shown are equally spaced for a uniform 4-bit ADC. Their corresponding analog voltage boundaries depend on the ADC input range.
Explanation

When an analog sample falls within a particular bin, the ADC assigns its corresponding digital level.

For example, the ADC may assign an analog measurement falling in bin 8 to output level 8.

Each sample is therefore represented by one of the 16 available amplitude levels.
Slide 50 — Sampling Versus Quantization
These operations must be clearly distinguished.

Sampling answers: At what times do we measure the waveform?

Quantization answers: What digital amplitude value do we assign to each measurement?

Visual example

Show the same signal in three successive stages:
1. Continuous analog waveform.
2. Analog measurements marked at equal time intervals.
3. Measured points assigned to available digital amplitude levels.
The third display should resemble a stepped collection of digital amplitude values rather than a continuous analog curve.
Slide 51 — Automatic Gain Control
Definition

Automatic Gain Control (AGC) adjusts the amplification applied to the signal so that the ADC's input voltage range is used appropriately.

If the signal entering the ADC is too small, only a few central quantization levels may be used.

If the signal is too large, the ADC cannot represent amplitudes beyond its range. This is known as clipping or saturation.

Visual example

Show three cases:
Input Condition	Quantization Result
Gain too low	Most samples occupy a few middle levels
Appropriate gain	Samples occupy a useful range of levels
Gain too high	Many samples accumulate at the highest and lowest levels

Slide 52 — Signal Condition After Digitization
Time-domain condition

The waveform is represented by discrete numerical amplitude values rather than a continuously varying voltage.

The sample values can only occupy the finite levels supported by the ADC.

Frequency-domain condition

The sampled signal is represented around a digital IF of 9.548 MHz for the reference design.

The wanted GNSS modulation is retained, while the sample representation also contains noise and any residual interference.

The ADC does not identify satellites or remove the thermal noise.

Section 09 — Resulting Digital GNSS Signal
Slide 53 — The Output of the GPS Front End
At the output of the ADC, the reference GPS front end produces digital IF samples described by three essential parameters:
\[
f_s=38.192\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
f_{\mathrm{digital}}=9.548\text{ MHz}
\]

\[
N=4\text{ bits per sample}
\]

These samples contain the received composite GNSS signal together with noise.

The receiver can now analyze them using digital signal-processing methods.
Slide 54 — Time-Domain Representation After Digitization
Definition

A digital time-domain plot shows the recorded sample amplitude against time or sample number.

The time-domain recording of a real GNSS signal is normally noise-like. Even though the digital IF is 9.548 MHz, the waveform does not necessarily show a clearly distinguishable sinusoid.

Example

Suppose 1,000 samples are displayed.

At 38.192 million samples per second, the displayed time span is approximately:
\[
T=\frac{1000}{38.192\times10^6}
\]

\[
T\approx26.2\ \mu s
\]

Visual example

Plot 1,000 discrete amplitude values with sample number on the horizontal axis and quantized amplitude on the vertical axis. Show the rapid irregular variation expected in a noise-dominated capture.
Slide 55 — Histogram of Digitized Samples
Definition

A histogram shows how many digital samples occupy each available amplitude level.

For a 4-bit ADC, there are 16 possible levels.

For a noise-dominated GNSS input with appropriate gain, the histogram will often resemble a bell-shaped distribution.

Example

Suppose 1,048,576 samples are recorded.

The histogram counts how many of these samples were assigned to each of the 16 amplitude levels.

The total count across all bins must equal the number of recorded samples.

Visual example
Illustrative Histogram — 4-Bit Quantized Samples
Conceptual, not measured data. Middle levels contain more samples than extreme levels, illustrating a typical noise-dominated amplitude distribution.
Interpretation

A balanced, centrally concentrated histogram indicates that the ADC levels are being used without excessive clipping.

A large accumulation at the extreme bins can indicate excessive gain.
Slide 56 — Frequency-Domain Representation After Digitization
Definition

The frequency-domain representation shows how power is distributed over the frequencies present in the digital sample sequence.

The reference output has a digital IF centered at approximately 9.548 MHz.

The GPS L1 C/A main-lobe bandwidth is approximately 2.046 MHz.

Visual example

Show the sampled spectrum with a frequency axis, a noise floor, and a central signal band near 9.548 MHz.

The desired central main lobe may appear as a broad rise in the spectrum when the combined received GNSS power and observation conditions make it visible.

The important point is that the frequency-domain representation can help identify the signal band, unwanted interference, and the effect of receiver filtering.
Slide 57 — Why the Received Samples Still Look Like Noise
The received GPS signal remains weak relative to thermal noise.

The antenna, filters, amplifiers, mixer, and ADC do not directly extract the PRN code from that noise.

The visible time-domain waveform can therefore appear random even when valid GNSS information is present.

The next receiver stages use the known satellite ranging codes to identify and process the received signals.

Interpretation

An irregular sampled waveform does not, by itself, mean that GNSS reception has failed.
Slide 58 — Three Representations of the Same Digitized Signal

Time Domain
Shows amplitude variation and clipping.
Histogram
Shows how the quantization levels are occupied.
Frequency Domain
Shows spectral content, including a possible main-lobe rise near the digital IF.


Each graph answers a different question.

Slide 60 — Final Worked Example: Follow One GPS L1 Signal
A GPS satellite transmits a code-modulated signal centered at 1575.42 MHz.

The antenna receives the weak signal and converts it into an electrical voltage. The front end then performs the following operations.
1. Reception: The antenna receives the weak GPS L1 signal, which is below the thermal noise floor in the reference example.
2. RF Filtering: The filter selects the wanted L1 band and attenuates unwanted out-of-band energy.
3. Amplification: The RF amplifier increases the weak signal magnitude while adding as little extra noise as practical.
4. Frequency Translation: The mixer combines the 1575.42 MHz RF signal with the 1527.68 MHz local oscillator, producing a desired 47.74 MHz IF.
5. IF Conditioning: Filtering and amplification prepare the analog signal for the ADC.
6. Sampling: The ADC measures the signal at 38.192 million samples per second. The desired IF appears at 9.548 MHz in the sampled spectrum.
7. Quantization: Each measured amplitude is represented using one of 16 possible levels in the 4-bit ADC.
8. Digital Output: The sampled signal can be represented by a time-domain waveform, amplitude histogram, and frequency-domain spectrum.
The final output is a digital representation of the combined received GNSS signals, suitable for subsequent acquisition and tracking.

For the actual lecture design, every front-end component should begin with the same full receive-chain diagram, with the currently discussed stage highlighted. The following example slides should use a consistent arrangement:

The most important design distinction is that idealized teaching waveforms must not be confused with real noise-dominated GNSS signals. Clean sinusoids are appropriate for explaining frequency, sampling, and mixing. Realistic noisy waveforms should be used to show the actual state of the received GPS signal.

The final digitization section should prominently include the analog waveform, sampling points, quantization bins, discrete digital waveform, 16-level histogram, and digital IF spectrum, in that order. This preserves the logical signal chain and makes the transition from electronics to digital GNSS signal processing understandable to students encountering these concepts for the first time.
